Why this is a decision

SPEC 6.4 fixes the words: "142 unchanged, 19 to update, 4 conflicts to review, 31 new," and it fixes that the GM "can accept the whole plan or walk it entity by entity." It does not fix how those four buckets are laid out or what "accept the whole plan" actually presses.

There is a trap here worth naming before drawing anything. Guardrail 1 says no accept-all default, and propagation caps at ten entries with no bulk option at all (SPEC 5.1). Yet import explicitly allows accepting a whole plan of dozens of entries in one click. The spec is not contradicting itself: unchanged entries are never proposals, they update silently because nothing of the user's is at stake (SPEC 6.4's merge policy). Updates and new entities are a bounded, freshly-computed plan the GM has already seen counted and can inspect before pressing anything. Conflicts are the one bucket where the user's own edit is on the line, and the merge policy is absolute: "never overwrite." A whole-plan accept that also swallows conflicts is not a shortcut, it is the exact failure guardrail 1 exists to prevent. The job of this page is to make that boundary visible, not to make it a rule the reader has to already know.

Guardrail 1 explicit accept, per entry, no accept-all default: satisfied here because "accept the whole plan" is one deliberate action over a bucket the GM has already inspected, and it is never offered for the conflicts bucket. That bucket is always walked, one by one.

Options

A

Four buckets, one card each

The plan is a small dashboard: a summary card, then one card per bucket. Unchanged and conflicts get no bulk button by construction; updates and new each get their own "accept all" scoped to that bucket only.

Buys

  • The bucket boundary from SPEC 6.4 is a boundary in the layout, not a rule the GM has to remember.
  • Scoped bulk actions ("accept all 19") are legible: the count on the button matches the count in the header.
  • A GM who only cares about conflicts can ignore three of the four cards entirely.

Costs

  • Four cards for 214 documents is a lot of vertical space before any single entity is visible.
  • The unchanged card, at 142 rows, has to stay collapsed or the page becomes a long scroll to nothing.
  • Walking "entity by entity" means jumping between cards rather than following one continuous list.
B

One list, filtered by chips

A single flat list of 214 rows. Four chips filter the view; walking is scrolling. Selection and bulk accept operate only on what is currently visible, and the conflicts chip disables the select-all control.

Buys

  • One list, one scroll position, one mental model for "walk it entity by entity" across all 214.
  • Chips double as the count summary, no separate dashboard card needed.
  • Filtering to conflicts alone is a fast way to find the 4 that matter most.

Costs

  • Safety now depends on the conflicts chip correctly disabling bulk select every time, a single CSS or state bug away from the exact overwrite guardrail 1 forbids.
  • 142 unchanged rows are technically in the list even if collapsed by default, tempting a "show all" that nobody should press.
  • Harder to see the shape of the whole plan at a glance; the chips carry the counts but not the relative weight.
C

Guided steps

A short wizard: unchanged is skipped automatically, then one step for updates, one for conflicts, one for new. Each step ends before the next begins.

Buys

  • Impossible to accidentally bulk-accept conflicts, the step simply never offers it.
  • Good for a first import where the GM genuinely wants to look at every bucket in order.
  • The "skipped" state on unchanged is explicit rather than implied by a collapsed card.

Costs

  • Forces a sequence on a GM who just wants to accept everything and go check the 4 conflicts, adding clicks for the common case.
  • No view of the whole plan at once, which fights "accept the whole plan" reading as one honest action rather than four small ones.
  • A wizard is the heaviest of the three to build and the easiest to make feel like a form, the exact tedium SPEC 4.2 warns against for a different surface.

Rejected outright

One "Accept everything" button covering all four buckets

It is the most literal reading of "accept the whole plan" and the most dangerous one. The merge policy table in SPEC 6.4 is unconditional for conflicts: "conflict, raise a proposal... never overwrite." A button that also resolves the 4 conflicts, silently taking the source version over the GM's own edit to The Gilded Rat's description, is Guardrail 1 broken in the one place it was written to stop: an edit applied without an explicit, informed accept.

What I would take

A, four buckets as cards

I would take A. The bucket boundary that matters, conflicts never get a bulk button, is a fact about the layout rather than a fact the GM has to trust the software got right. B is more elegant for a GM who lives in lists, but it puts the safety property in application state (does the chip correctly disable select-all) instead of in the DOM, which is a worse place for a guardrail to live. C is the safest of all three but adds ceremony most re-imports do not need, since most re-imports after the first are mostly unchanged and a few updates.

Cheapest way to find out this is wrong: build A first, then run the CI fixture from SPEC 6.4 (import the same export twice, expect zero changes on the second run) against it and watch whether the "142 unchanged" card ever has to render anything but a count. If unchanged entities keep leaking into the updates bucket, the bug is in the matcher (#37), not the layout, and no redesign here fixes it.

What this locks in

  • #37 the bucket a document lands in depends entirely on the matcher's confidence and the merge policy computed from it; this page assumes that split is already correct before anything is drawn.
  • #36 every entity in the plan needs its source reference (system, external id, content hash) resolved before the dry run renders, since that is what separates "unchanged" from "to update" in the first place.
  • Hard to undo: once "accept the whole plan" ships as a button that explicitly excludes conflicts, weakening that later, offering a bulk action that includes them, reopens a guardrail question that should stay closed.
  • Deferred on purpose: what the conflict review screen itself looks like, which is D5; how 200 new proposals from a first import (no prior plan to diff against) get reviewed at scale, which is D4.
