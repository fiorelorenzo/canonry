Why this is a decision

SPEC 14 lists seven metrics and says two decide the product: accept rate of propagation proposals, and time from import to first accepted proposal. A third, acceptance ratio per import, is explicitly instrumented per playbook rather than in aggregate, because a playbook that produces noise is a maintenance problem the average hides. None of that says who is allowed to see the numbers. The honest tension is that a GM's own accept rate is genuinely useful feedback and also a strange thing to show a customer: nobody wants to feel graded on how often they trusted the tool they are paying for, and the tool should not have an incentive to nudge that number up by, say, softening its own proposals.

Guardrail 3 every proposal shows its evidence, never an arbitrary confidence score. A summary metric shown back to a GM risks becoming exactly that score, one level up, if it is not handled carefully.

Since decided

H1 gives the admin surface decided here, staff-only, inside the product, reusing its own auth, its first real room: /admin/pricing, one row per operation in operation_price, editable with the change taking effect immediately and writing an audit row in operation_price_change. It reuses exactly what B already argued for, the app's own chrome and role check rather than a second tool or a second auth mechanism, so nothing about this decision needed revisiting to fit it: pricing is one more staff-only table next to the metrics one drawn below, not a new kind of surface.

Options

All three options render the same underlying numbers: only who can reach the screen changes.

A

A separate internal tool

Not reachable from the product at all. A different, unstyled surface staff opens on its own subdomain.

Buys

  • Cannot leak into the product's own UI by accident: no shared components, no shared auth to misconfigure.
  • Free to be blunt: raw tables, no product polish required.
  • Easy to restrict at the infrastructure level, not just with a role check.

Costs

  • A second surface to build, deploy and keep working: the exact kind of extra container SPEC 12 spent effort avoiding elsewhere.
  • Staff have to context-switch out of the product to check it, which in practice means it gets checked less.
  • No reuse of the app's own auth, so "who is staff" has to be re-solved here.
B

An admin surface inside the product

Same tables, same numbers, but behind a staff-only route inside the app, reusing its chrome and its auth.

Buys

  • Reuses the app's own role and auth system instead of inventing a second one.
  • Staff are already logged into the product; checking metrics is one navigation away, not a context switch.
  • Shares components with every other table in the app (D3's dry run, F2's usage rows), so it costs less to build than a bespoke internal tool.

Costs

  • A staff-only route living inside the product is one misconfigured guard away from a leak: the boundary is a role check, not a separate deployment.
  • Ties the internal tool's release cadence to the product's, instead of letting it move independently.
  • Tempting to add "just one more" GM-facing chart here later, blurring the line this decision is trying to hold.
C

Partly shown to the GM

The GM's own account gets a small personal panel. Cross-GM aggregates and per-playbook detail stay internal-only.

Buys

  • Genuinely useful feedback: a GM who rejects almost everything might realise their world is odd rather than the copilot being bad, or the reverse.
  • Transparency about the tool's own limits sits well with guardrail 7: a visible accept rate is closer to "here is what doesn't add up" than a hidden one.
  • Costs little to add once B exists: a scoped query, not a new surface.

Costs

  • The argument against is not hypothetical: an accept rate shown to the person being measured by it is a strange incentive on both sides: the GM may start accepting to keep their number up, and the product may be tempted to soften proposals to keep GMs looking successful.
  • A percentage handed to a customer with no context reads like a grade, which is adjacent to the arbitrary confidence score guardrail 3 forbids at the proposal level.
  • Once shipped, removing it reads as taking away a feature, even if it was never healthy for anyone's incentives.

Rejected outright

A public aggregate accept rate, as a trust signal

"GMs accept 83% of what the Loremaster proposes" reads as marketing but functions as exactly the arbitrary confidence score Guardrail 3 forbids, just moved from the proposal to the whole product. It risks being read as permission to stop reviewing carefully, which is the specific failure Guardrail 1 exists to prevent by refusing an accept-all default in the first place.

A grade or score for the GM's own accept rate

A letter grade, a streak counter, or any framing that turns the GM's own number into a score to keep up is the same failure aimed at the customer instead of the product: Guardrail 3 says never an arbitrary confidence score, and a gamified personal metric is one dressed differently.

What I would take

B, and defer C until there is real data to argue from

Take B. Reusing the product's own auth and components is strictly cheaper than standing up a separate tool, and per-playbook granularity (SPEC 14 point 6) is what actually drives decisions (which playbook needs work) so it belongs where the people who act on it already are. C is a real, defensible idea and I would not build it yet: the incentive problem it raises is not solvable by design alone, it needs to be watched. Ship B, watch whether a low playbook accept rate ever gets fixed faster because staff can see it easily, and revisit whether GMs would benefit from their own number once there is a year of internal data to know what a healthy range even looks like.

Cheapest way to find out this is wrong: check whether staff actually open the admin route inside the product, or keep asking for a numbers pull in Slack anyway. If nobody opens it, a weekly digest is cheaper to maintain than a UI at all, and B should become an export, not a screen.

What this locks in

  • #100 the accept-rate and time-to-first-accept queries (SPEC 4.4: proposal's outcome column) get built as first-class, not derived later from logs: the admin screen is the forcing function.
  • #101 per-playbook breakdown is the unit of the acceptance-ratio metric from day one, never an aggregate with playbook as an afterthought filter.
  • #103 whatever ships for staff reuses the app's role system rather than a second auth mechanism, which the admin-inside-the-product choice makes mandatory.
  • Deferred, on purpose: the GM-facing personal panel from C. Revisiting it needs a real answer to the incentive question, not just a design for the screen.
