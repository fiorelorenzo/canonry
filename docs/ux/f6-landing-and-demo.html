Why this is a decision

Issues #3 and #4 are the front door: the sentence that decides whether a skeptical GM keeps reading, and the demo that has to prove the one thing every other AI worldbuilding tool skips: that nothing gets written without a yes. This page carries more risk per word than any other surface in the product, because it is read by people who have not yet seen the accept button work and have every reason from the rest of this hobby to assume it will not. Overclaim by one sentence and the product spends its first year fighting its own landing page; underclaim and the beachhead (the roughly 30% of GMs who already use generative AI in prep (SPEC 2)) scrolls past looking for the copilot they were promised.

Guardrail 7 never promise consistency; contradiction detection sits at F1 ≈ 52%, so the page may say "here is what does not add up" and never "your canon is consistent". Guardrail 1 propose, never apply: so the demo may not show text appearing by itself, because that is not what the product does and the page's whole job is to not lie about that.

Options

Every option below uses only headline and subhead candidates that survive both guardrails. The full candidate list, including what was cut and why:

Headline candidateVerdict
"Change one thing. See everything it touches. Accept what's right." keep
"A copilot for your world that never writes without your yes." keep
"Your world, written down properly, with an AI that asks first." keep
"AI that keeps your whole world consistent." rejected
"Set it and forget it: your wiki writes itself." rejected
Subhead candidateVerdict
"Canonry is a wiki for your game world with a copilot built in: it drafts, flags what doesn't add up, and always asks before it writes." keep
"Import from Obsidian, Kanka, World Anvil, PDF or DOCX in minutes. Every change it proposes, you accept or reject, one entry at a time." keep
"Not a consistency checker. A second pair of eyes that shows you what changed and lets you decide." keep
"It keeps your whole campaign perfectly consistent, automatically." rejected
A

Static screenshot of the accept moment

Headline and subhead above a single frame: the diff review mid-accept, Aldric Vane, entry 3 of 7.

Buys

  • Cheapest to build and keep current: one mock, no animation or interaction to maintain.
  • Works everywhere, including a social-share preview or a no-JS fallback.
  • The Accept/Reject buttons are the visual focal point of the one image, so the most important piece of copy needs no separate explanation.

Costs

  • Static means accept is implied, not demonstrated: a skeptical visitor has to trust the screenshot rather than see the click happen.
  • Skips the "why": no plan, no "this touches 7 entries", so a first-time visitor does not see where the number 7 in "entry 3 of 7" came from.
  • Ages the moment the real product's visual language (A1) ships and diverges from this exact mock.
B

Animated three-step loop

Headline and subhead above a hero that auto-cycles three complete frames: edit, plan, diff-and-accept. No frame ever shows text being typed.

Buys

  • Shows the whole loop to a visitor who is scrolling, not reading: the plan before the diff is exactly the mechanism competitors don't have.
  • Three complete frames, never simulated typing, so nothing here shows text appearing by itself.
  • One hero serves both a skimmer and someone willing to watch a full cycle.

Costs

  • Autoplay risks reading as "the AI just doing it" through motion alone, even with complete, non-typing frames: the eye reads movement as autonomy regardless of intent.
  • Needs a pause control and a reduced-motion fallback to be accessible, which is more to build and test than a static frame.
  • A moving hero is one more thing that can look broken (a stuck frame, a layout shift) on a page whose entire pitch is trustworthiness.
C

The demo as the hero, no copy above it

One small line naming the product. Everything else above the fold is the real three-step demo, clickable, with a real accept step.

Buys

  • The most literal reading of guardrail 1 available on a marketing page: nobody reads the word "accept" without also seeing the button, because clicking it is how the demo moves forward.
  • Speaks directly to the beachhead: GMs who already use AI tools want to see the mechanism, not be sold to first.
  • The accept step is a real click with a real state change, not implied trust in a screenshot.

Costs

  • A visitor with no context and no copy above the fold may not know what they're looking at before they start clicking.
  • Gives up the space that would otherwise carry the single strongest sentence on the page.
  • Degrades worse than A on a slow connection or with JS disabled: a static image still communicates, a dead tab switcher does not.

Rejected outright

"AI that keeps your whole world consistent."

Contradiction detection sits at F1 ≈ 52% (SPEC 5.2). Promising consistency is a direct violation of Guardrail 7, which requires the product to say "here is what does not add up" and never certify that anything is consistent. This headline is also simply untrue about half the time, which is worse than a guardrail violation on its own.

"Set it and forget it: your wiki writes itself." / "It keeps your whole campaign perfectly consistent, automatically."

Both describe a product that applies changes without a human, which Guardrail 1 forbids outright. Canonry never writes without an explicit per-entry accept; copy that implies otherwise sets an expectation the product is built specifically not to meet, and the first propagation review a new GM sees will feel like a bait and switch.

A hero that types the diff out on screen, letter by letter, before any accept

Even where the underlying product never applies anything without a click, an animation that shows text appearing by itself visually asserts the opposite of Guardrail 1 on the one page whose entire job is to state the product honestly. A viewer remembers the motion, not the disclaimer next to it.

What I would take

C, with the one-line descriptor kept, nothing more restored above it

Take C. The single most important piece of copy in the repo is not a sentence, it is a button, and only C puts the actual Accept click above the fold where the beachhead audience: GMs already comfortable with AI tools, who want to see the mechanism before they read a pitch, will find it in the first few seconds. Keep the one-line descriptor exactly as drawn; dropping it entirely risks a visitor not knowing what they're interacting with, which is a real cost, but a full headline-and-subhead block above the demo would just recreate A's problem of describing the accept step instead of letting someone perform it.

Below the fold, regardless of which hero option ships, this paragraph belongs on the page and is not part of the A/B/C choice above: "Canonry is built around a language model, and we say so here instead of after you sign up. If you already use ChatGPT or Claude to help run your game, about a third of GMs do, this is that habit with a memory of your world and an accept button instead of copy-paste. If the AI debate you've heard is about art replacing illustrators in published books, that's a different argument: the portraits here are for your own table, never published, and the whole feature switches off if you'd rather not use it: the wiki still works without it." That last clause is guardrail 4 stated as a promise to a visitor, not just an engineering property.

Cheapest way to find out this is wrong: ship C and watch two numbers: the click-through rate on the three demo tabs, and whether the CTA below the demo gets clicked without a visitor ever touching a tab. If most visitors never interact with the demo at all, the bare-demo bet failed and a headline needs to come back above the fold, closer to option A.

What this locks in

  • #3 the headline and subhead set above become the literal source of truth for the pitch; any future marketing copy is checked against this record, not drafted fresh by whoever writes it next.
  • #4 the demo's exact three steps (edit, plan, diff-and-accept) become the reference sequence for any future embedded product tour, and the fact that Accept and Reject are real, clickable, and disable themselves after one use sets the expectation that marketing demos behave like the product in miniature, not a staged animation.
  • Hard to undo: once the "about a third of GMs" framing and the "art replacing illustrators" line ship, walking them back reads as the product backing away from its own beachhead strategy (SPEC 2), which is a bigger reversal than a copy edit.
  • Deferred: real product screenshots replace these mocks once A1 (visual language) and C4 (diff presentation) ship. Deferred: any language beyond English.
