Why this is a decision

§4.4 fixes the model: revelation records what the players discovered and in which session, and §10 fixes what it drives: the players' wiki filter is a join on that table, never a per-entry flag someone remembers to flip. What is not fixed is the grain the GM marks things at, and grain is a real cost during play, in seconds and in attention, not a modelling nicety. Get this wrong and either #82's reveal action is too fussy to survive a real session and gets skipped, or it is too coarse and the players' wiki (E7) shows more than actually came up.

Guardrail 6 nothing unreviewed is ever published to players. A reveal interaction is the GM's own review step for what the party knows, whatever grain is chosen, it has to be one the GM will actually perform, or the guardrail is only as strong as their memory.

Options

A

Per entry, live

A "mark as revealed" toggle on the pinned card itself. One tap, the whole entry becomes visible in the players' wiki.

Buys

  • Cheapest live action of the three: one tap, immediate, no screen change, no leaving the scene.
  • Matches how a table actually plays: the GM reacts to what was just said, in the moment.
  • The reveal is visible instantly if a player checks the wiki on their own phone mid-session.

Costs

  • Coarse: revealing The Ashen Ledger's entry exposes every accepted fact on it, not just "who Aldric answers to".
  • Still a tap the GM has to remember to make, for every single thing that comes up, all session.
  • A missed tap under pressure just does not happen; there is no second chance built in.
B

Per fact or sentence

Open the entry, select the specific sentence that was said aloud, mark that fact revealed. Precise, and it costs what precision costs.

Buys

  • The most precise match to what actually happened: exactly the sentence spoken, nothing more.
  • An entry can be "partially known", the players' wiki can show a real, honest partial page.
  • Closest fit to the domain model's fact table, which already carries a span.

Costs

  • Four taps and up to twenty seconds per fact does not survive a scene with six things said aloud.
  • Requires leaving the pinned-card view and opening the full entry, which table mode exists to avoid.
  • A GM who has to hunt for the sentence mid-dialogue will simply stop doing this by session three.
C

Session log, confirmed afterwards

During play: nothing required, an optional one-tap flag. After the session: a pre-populated checklist of what was touched, confirmed in one pass.

Buys

  • Zero required cost during play. Nothing competes with actually running the scene.
  • One batch pass, at the GM's own pace, catches things they meant to reveal but forgot mid-scene.
  • Mirrors the propagation accept flow (C3, C6): a reviewable plan, not a live commitment.

Costs

  • A player checking the wiki on their phone mid-session sees nothing update until after the table breaks.
  • The pre-filled list is only as good as what session_context tracked; an unpinned mention is missed entirely.
  • Adds a small end-of-session ritual, which is one more thing a tired GM can decide to skip.

Rejected outright

A manual public/private flag on the entry, set during prep

The most familiar pattern from every wiki tool on the market: an entry has a visibility switch, the GM flips it when they feel like it. §4.4 and §10 reject this by name: revelation exists precisely so the filter is a join on session-linked evidence, not "a per-entry flag someone has to remember to flip." It fails in both directions at once: forget to flip it on and the party is stuck unable to read about something they were told at the table; forget to flip it off, set it early during prep to save a step, and it leaks a fact nobody has said yet. Guardrail 6 nothing unreviewed reaches players. A flag with no session behind it is not a review, it is a guess about the future.

What I would take

C, with A as an escape hatch

Take C. Zero required cost during play beats a one-tap cost during play, every time a table is mid-scene, and the after-session batch review turns "did I reveal everything that came up" into a single confirm pass instead of a memory test. B's fact-level precision is real and worth keeping as what the checklist operates on under the hood: the session log can propose specific facts, not whole entries. But asking the GM to pick sentences live is asking too much of a table. I would keep A's live tap available for the rare theatrical reveal, the moment the GM wants the players' wiki to update while everyone is still looking at their phones.

Cheapest way to find out this is wrong: ship the optional live flag from C first, alone, before the batch screen exists, and see how often GMs use it unprompted. If most sessions end with zero flags and several things clearly discovered, the end-of-session list needs to be built from session_context automatically rather than relying on the live flag at all, which is exactly the design C already assumes.

What this locks in

  • #82 ships the revelation writer as a batch confirm tied to session_context, not a single live toggle. The after-session screen is the primary surface, not an afterthought bolted onto the pin card.
  • Locks that revelation rows can target a fact, not only an entity, so the batch screen can offer "this sentence" as well as "this whole entry" without a schema change later.
  • Locks the players' wiki (E7) to updating asynchronously, after the table breaks, for the common case. Live updates during play are the exception, not the default to design around.
  • Deferred: how "already known" is shown across sessions on the pin card itself belongs to the pinned-card component from E1 and E2, reused here rather than redesigned.
