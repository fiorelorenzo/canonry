Why this is a decision

SPEC 4.3 makes a work (oneshot, module, campaign, story, novel). Its own thing: an ordered tree of act, chapter, scene, encounter, separate from the universe because it has a different shape and lifecycle. The link that makes it worth having runs both ways: work_node_entity records which entries a scene uses, so changing Aldric Vane tells you scene 3 of chapter 2 is affected, and whatever happens while writing or playing flows back into the universe as proposals, never as a direct write. This page is only about the first half: how a GM edits a work next to the canon it draws on, and how "this scene depends on an entry that just changed" gets in front of them.

Get the layout wrong and one of two things happens. Either the tree drowns a long campaign like Debts of Valdoria in navigation and the actual prose nobody ever gets to, or the "this scene uses Aldric Vane" signal grows accept and reject buttons of its own and becomes a second place proposals get decided.

The propagate-back half of 4.3 is Guardrail 1's territory: one explicit accept, per entry, and C2 owns where that happens. If a scene's "uses" list also carries a Confirm button, the product now has two places that can each believe they hold the current answer for the same proposal, and the accept-rate instrumentation SPEC 4.4 depends on stops being a single, trustworthy count. So every option below treats the freshness signal as read and link, never review.

Options

A

Tree beside a scene editor

The familiar three-pane shape the rest of the app already uses: the work's tree on the left, the open scene's text in the middle, its canon dependencies on the right. One scene open at a time, but the whole tree stays reachable.

Buys

  • Same shape as every entry page in the app; nothing new to learn to start writing a scene.
  • The tree scales cleanly to four levels (act, chapter, scene, encounter) without the outline needing to nest visually.
  • The editor pane gets full width for actual prose, which is most of what prep time is.

Costs

  • Three panes is a lot of a laptop screen; on anything narrower, one of them has to go first.
  • Only one scene open at a time, so checking whether scene 2 also depends on Aldric means switching away from scene 3.
  • A campaign with many acts makes the left rail tall enough to need its own scroll and, eventually, search.
B

An outline that expands in place

One column. Acts and chapters collapse to a line; opening a scene expands its text and its uses directly underneath, and its siblings stay in view above and below it.

Buys

  • No context switch: opening scene 3 does not hide scene 2 or chapter 1.
  • Works on a narrow screen without dropping a pane, which the three-column shape cannot do below 900px.
  • Comparing two open scenes side by side is just scrolling, not two separate views.

Costs

  • The uses row is horizontal and short on room, so the "why" text is the first thing that gets cut on a longer relation.
  • A chapter with several scenes open at once gets tall fast, and a long campaign's whole outline never fits one screen.
  • Four levels of indentation reads fine for act/chapter/scene, but a scene's own encounter nodes are one nesting level further than this pattern comfortably holds.
C

A board of scenes

Chapters as columns, scenes as cards. Built for looking at a whole chapter at once before a session, not for writing prose. Opening a card is a link out to the editor, not an edit in place.

Buys

  • The best view of "what's changed across this whole act" before a session, which is exactly the moment this signal matters most.
  • Every scene's freshness is visible at once, not one at a time behind a click.
  • Reads like session prep already looks on paper: a chapter, its scenes, at a glance.

Costs

  • Not an editor. Writing a scene's actual text still means a click-through, so the board is a router wearing an editor's clothes.
  • Encounter nodes have no natural column; they would need to be a filter or a separate view rather than a place on the board.
  • A campaign with many chapters scrolls sideways, which degrades faster than a tree or an outline scrolls down.

Rejected outright

The uses list carries its own Confirm and Reject

It reads as a convenience: the GM is already looking at the scene, so let them clear the Aldric change right there. It is exactly the second proposal inbox this decision has to avoid. Guardrail 1 asks for one explicit accept per entry, not one explicit accept per place the entry happens to be mentioned, and §14's accept-rate metric stops meaning anything the moment two surfaces can each register an outcome for the same proposal. The fix costs nothing: the chip links to the entry or to C2's review, and stays read-only everywhere else.

Scenes as free text with no entity links

The cheapest thing to build: a scene is a heading and a textarea, same as any notes app. It is also work_node_entity deleted by omission, and without it this whole decision has nothing to draw. There is no way to know a scene depends on Aldric Vane at all, let alone that he changed four minutes ago. That is not a smaller version of the feature, it is Obsidian with extra steps, and Obsidian is the incumbent SPEC §2 says this product has to beat on exactly this point.

What I would take

A, tree beside the editor

I would take A. It costs nothing against the shape the rest of the app already commits to for reading and editing something long-form next to structured metadata, which is exactly what a scene is: prose plus a short list of entities. Debts of Valdoria is a running campaign with real depth in its tree, and A is the only option that keeps all four levels reachable without either drowning the page in indentation (B) or losing the ability to write the scene's own text in place at all (C).

Cheapest way to find out this is wrong: build this after table mode (E1) and watch whether GMs actually open the work tree during a session, versus only ever during prep. If play-time use is real, that is a case for something closer to C's glanceable board as a companion view, not a reason to change how the scene itself gets written.

What this locks in

  • #20 the work model needs an efficient reverse lookup (which scenes reference entity X). Not just a top-down tree read, because every option above depends on answering "who uses Aldric Vane" cheaply enough to run on every page load.
  • #20 also needs a rule for what counts as "changed": since the GM last opened this node, since the last session, or a fixed rolling window. This page assumes "since last opened" but does not decide it.
  • Deferred to C2: what actually happens when a GM clicks through from a stale dependency. This page only promises a link, not a destination.
  • Deferred to E1: whether a work needs a second, play-time view distinct from this prep-time one. Nothing here rules it out.
  • Hard to undo: A commits "Works" to its own three-pane shape, distinct from an entry page's, which is a second layout the app has to keep consistent as it grows rather than a variation on the first.
