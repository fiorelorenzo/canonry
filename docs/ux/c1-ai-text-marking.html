Why this is a decision

Every propagation run and every completed field puts unaccepted text next to written text on the same page. The GM has to tell them apart at a glance, forever, because the whole loop depends on nobody mistaking a proposal for canon. What the spec does not fix is how loud that marking gets to be. Pick the wrong answer by accident and either the entry looks fine for a one-word change and unreadable for a real rewrite, or it looks safely muted and a GM skims past a proposal thinking it is already theirs.

Guardrail 2 AI text is visually distinct until accepted, and stays tracked afterwards. "Visually distinct" is doing the real work in that sentence: distinct for a reader who cannot rely on hue, not only for the reader who can. A treatment that only works in full colour vision is not visually distinct, it is visually distinct for most people.

Two constraints outside this page shape it anyway. A1 reserves violet as the one hue that means "not yours yet", in both the light and the dark palette, so whatever marking wins here has to read correctly against both sets of tokens. And C2 decides propagation runs in the background after a save, which means the marking has to survive being noticed minutes or hours later, cold, not in the middle of watching it happen.

Options

A

Tint and rule

The proposed sentence sits inline, tinted violet with a left rule, immediately after the old sentence struck through. What is being asked for is shown in place, in full, as part of the paragraph.

Buys

  • Shows the exact proposed replacement without leaving the entry, so a one-sentence change is legible at a glance.
  • Reuses the provisional .ai-text class already in ux.css, so choosing A as-is costs no new component.
  • The left rule is a border, not only a fill, so it still reads under a grayscale filter.

Costs

  • A four-sentence rewrite becomes four struck-through and tinted pairs stacked in one paragraph. See the worst case below.
  • Shows the proposed wording as if it already belonged to the entry, which risks a skimming GM reading unaccepted text as canon.
  • Strikethrough plus tint roughly doubles the reading length of every touched paragraph.
B

Underline and margin marker

The entry keeps its own written text, untouched. A dashed underline marks which sentence has a pending proposal, and one numbered marker per paragraph says how many. The proposed wording itself lives in the diff, not in the entry.

Buys

  • The entry never shows text that is not yet true; the GM reads what they wrote, marked, not rewritten.
  • One marker per paragraph, not per sentence, so a four-sentence proposal is exactly as visible as a one-sentence one.
  • A dashed underline and a numbered marker both carry meaning without colour, so it holds under deuteranopia.

Costs

  • Adds a click between "I noticed something" and "I can read the proposal", which a one-sentence change may not need.
  • The marker needs a gutter the entry does not otherwise reserve, which competes with a docked sidebar for room.
  • A GM who never opens a marker gets no visible value from the copilot even though it did the work.
C

Layered card

The entry is never mutated. A card carrying the full proposed paragraph floats above it, offset like a note dropped on a page, with accept and reject on the card itself.

Buys

  • The entry reads exactly as written even mid-proposal, a direct answer to guardrail 4's "AI off still looks fine" test.
  • Accept and reject sit on the card itself, closer to the decision than a separate proposal queue.
  • The offset and shadow read as "not yet here" by position and depth, not colour, at any contrast setting.

Costs

  • Three stacked cards for three touched paragraphs push the entry's height up before any of them is read.
  • Easy to visually skip on a busy screen, which cuts against guardrail 2's "unmistakable".
  • Needs its own responsive behaviour at narrow entry widths that B1 has not settled yet.

The worst case, together: a proposal that rewrites all four sentences of one paragraph, the same three treatments, side by side, dark palette (the per-option tabs above already cover light). Original wording is expanded from the entries table for Aldric Vane, Iselde Wrenn and the Valdoria Watch, reused verbatim from A1's mock.

Rejected outright

Colour alone: violet text, no background, no border, no shape change

It is the cheapest possible implementation of "distinct" and the first thing a developer reaches for when the token exists. It also fails Guardrail 2 for a colour-blind reader: violet-on-ink can sit close enough to the default link colour under deuteranopia that "AI text" and "a mention chip" become the same grey smear. "Visually distinct" has to mean distinct for every reader, not for the majority. Every option above pairs the hue with a border, an underline shape, or a position offset for exactly this reason.

What I would take

B, underline and margin marker

Take B. It is the only treatment that never puts unaccepted wording into the entry's own reading flow, which matters more than it looks: A and C both show the proposed sentence as if it already belonged to the paragraph, and a GM skimming their own canon at reading speed, not review speed, is exactly the reader guardrail 2 is protecting. B keeps the entry saying only what the GM actually wrote, and moves the proposed wording to the one place it is read deliberately: the diff, decided in C4. The worst-case row above shows why this matters at scale too: A's four stacked strike-and-tint pairs are close to unreadable as prose, B's single "4" marker is not, and C is the only real competitor, at the cost of a card that has to find room on every touched paragraph.

Cheapest way to find out this is wrong: put a real four-sentence propagation proposal (the Valdoria Watch worst case above is representative) in front of a GM who has never seen the marker before, and watch whether they notice it at all during normal reading, or whether it needs to be louder, with a bigger marker, or a top-of-entry banner before it earns the word "unmistakable".

What this locks in

  • #106 ships the marker CSS and the paragraph-level counting logic described here, not a placeholder tint. The provisional .ai-text class already in ux.css is retired once this ships, since B does not use it for the marking itself.
  • #66 inherits the same vocabulary for a different content type: a generated portrait gets the same violet hue and a badge shape rather than its own convention, so "AI, not yet yours" means one thing across text and media.
  • Hard to undo: once a GM learns "dashed underline plus a number" means proposal pending, changing the shape later breaks a habit, not just a stylesheet.
  • Deferred on purpose: how the marking changes once a proposal is accepted; guardrail 2 also requires accepted AI text to "stay tracked". That belongs to B4, where facts, spans and revision provenance are designed against the full authorship model, not two paragraphs of fixture.
